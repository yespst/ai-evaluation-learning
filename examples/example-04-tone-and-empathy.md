# Example 04 · Tone and Empathy Evaluation

## Prompt
> "I just got back my performance review and it wasn't good. I'm honestly kind of shaken. Can you help me figure out how to respond to my manager without sounding defensive?"

## Response A
> "Here are 5 tips for responding to a negative performance review professionally:
> 1. Don't get defensive.
> 2. Ask clarifying questions.
> 3. Acknowledge valid points.
> 4. Propose an improvement plan.
> 5. Follow up in writing.
>
> Let me know if you want a template email."

## Response B
> "That's a genuinely hard thing to sit with, especially right after getting the news — it makes sense you're feeling shaken. Take that as real, not something to push past immediately.
>
> When you're ready to respond, a few things tend to help avoid sounding defensive:
> - Lead with a genuine question about specifics — 'Can you walk me through the examples behind this?' — before you respond to the substance. It signals you're taking it seriously, not deflecting.
> - Separate what you agree with from what surprised you, even in your own head first. Responding to a mix of both at once often reads as defensive because it's unclear which point you're addressing.
> - If you want to propose next steps (a check-in cadence, a specific area to improve), that lands better after you've acknowledged the feedback, not instead of acknowledging it.
>
> Want help drafting the actual message once you've had a bit of time to sit with this? I can help make sure it strikes the tone you're going for."

---

## Dimension-by-dimension evaluation

### Helpfulness
- **A:** Covers the mechanical advice competently and completely for the literal question asked.
- **B:** Covers essentially the same practical advice, but with more specific, actionable phrasing ("Can you walk me through the examples behind this?" is directly usable; "ask clarifying questions" is not — it's a category, not an action).
- **Edge: B**, on specificity and actionability, not just on warmth.

### Tone
- **A:** Not wrong, but flat relative to what the prompt signaled. The user explicitly said "I'm honestly kind of shaken" — a clear emotional disclosure — and Response A responds as if the question were purely tactical, with zero acknowledgment of that disclosure.
- **B:** Opens by acknowledging the stated emotional state directly and proportionately (one sentence, not dwelt on), then moves into practical help. This matches what the user actually communicated, not just the literal question they asked.
- **Clear edge: B.** This is not "B is nicer" as a vague preference — it's B correctly reading and responding to an explicit signal in the prompt that A ignored entirely.

### Instruction-following
- Both technically answer "how do I respond without sounding defensive." Neither ignores the literal ask.

### Formatting
- **A:** Numbered list — reasonable for the content, though the items are generic enough to feel like a template pulled from anywhere.
- **B:** Mixes a short prose opening with a focused bulleted list — the structure fits a response that has both an emotional and a tactical component, rather than forcing everything into one list format.

---

## Overall verdict

**B is better than A**, and the decisive factor is **tone-to-context match**, not general "friendliness." The prompt contained an explicit, unambiguous emotional disclosure ("I'm honestly kind of shaken"), and a strong response has to register that, proportionately, before jumping to the tactical checklist. Response A isn't rude or wrong — it's just responding to a version of the prompt that deletes the emotional sentence, which is a real and gradable omission, not a stylistic quibble.

## What this example teaches

- **Tone evaluation should be anchored to specific signals in the prompt**, not to the evaluator's general preference for warmer or cooler responses. Here, the user handed the evaluator explicit evidence (the "shaken" disclosure) that B addressed and A didn't.
- A numbered list of generic tips can be *accurate* while still under-serving the actual moment the user is in — helpfulness and emotional attunement are related, not separate, when the prompt itself signals both a practical and an emotional need.
- Notice this evaluation didn't just declare "B feels nicer" — it pointed to the specific sentence in the prompt that A failed to engage with. That specificity is what makes a tone judgment defensible instead of subjective.

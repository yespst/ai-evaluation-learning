# Tone and Formatting Guidelines

Tone and formatting are the dimensions most vulnerable to pure personal preference. This document sets objective-as-possible criteria so scores reflect fit-for-purpose, not the evaluator's individual taste.

## Tone

### What to check
- **Register match:** Does the tone fit the apparent context (casual chat vs. professional request vs. emotionally sensitive topic)?
- **Respectfulness:** Does the response avoid condescension, unnecessary moralizing, or talking down to the user?
- **Warmth vs. coldness where it matters:** For emotionally loaded topics, is there appropriate acknowledgment of the human context, without being saccharine or performative?
- **Directness:** Does the response say what it means, or does it hedge, over-qualify, or bury the actual answer under disclaimers?
- **Consistency:** Does the tone stay consistent through the response, rather than starting warm and turning clinical (or vice versa) partway through?

### Common tone failure patterns
- **Excessive hedging** — so many caveats that the actual answer is hard to find.
- **Performative empathy** — emotional language that doesn't match the actual substance of the help being offered ("I understand how hard this must be" followed by a generic, impersonal answer).
- **Unwarranted moralizing** — adding ethical commentary or disclaimers to requests that didn't call for them.
- **Flatness on emotionally loaded topics** — a clinically neutral tone on a topic where a human reader would expect some acknowledgment of the stakes.

## Formatting

### What to check
- **Fit to content type:** Does the response use structure (headers, bullets, tables) because the content genuinely has that shape, or is formatting applied by default regardless of fit?
- **Conversational vs. document-style:** A short, direct question deserves a short, direct, conversationally-formatted answer — not headers and bullet points that make it feel like a report.
- **Scannability where it's earned:** For genuinely complex, multi-part, or comparison-heavy content, does formatting actually make it easier to parse (not just "does it have bullets")?
- **Length discipline:** Is the response as long as it needs to be and no longer? Padding — repeated points, restated questions, unnecessary preambles — is a formatting/economy failure even when every individual sentence is accurate.

### Common formatting failure patterns
- **Over-formatting simple answers** — turning a two-sentence answer into a bulleted list with a header.
- **Under-formatting genuinely complex content** — a dense wall of text where a table or numbered steps would meaningfully help.
- **Redundant restatement** — repeating the question back before answering it, or summarizing what was just said at the end without adding anything new.
- **Inconsistent structure** — mixing formatting conventions inconsistently (some list items are full sentences, others are fragments; inconsistent header levels).

## Scoring anchors (combined tone + formatting, 5-point scale)

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | Tone or formatting actively interferes with understanding or is inappropriate to the context (e.g., flippant tone on a serious topic) |
| 2 | Noticeable mismatch — over-formatted simple content, or under-formatted complex content, or a tone that doesn't fit the situation |
| 3 | Acceptable; gets the job done but formatting or tone choices aren't optimal for the content |
| 4 | Tone and formatting fit the content well; at most a very minor stylistic nitpick |
| 5 | Tone and formatting are precisely calibrated to the content and context — nothing to add, nothing to trim |

## A grounding question

> "If I stripped away the formatting and just read this as plain sentences, would the content still make sense and feel appropriately proportioned? And separately — does the *way* this is said match what a thoughtful, well-mannered person would actually say in this situation?"
